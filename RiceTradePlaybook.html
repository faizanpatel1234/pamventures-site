1

Decide your role in the deal

In plain words

Rice going from India to the US has two sides: the India side (selling and exporting) and the US side (buying and importing). Your very first choice is which side you're on. It decides which licenses you need and where your risk sits.

  • Exporter (India side) — you buy from Indian mills and sell to a US buyer. You need the Indian licenses.
  • Importer (US side) — you open a US company and buy from an Indian exporter. You need the US licenses.
  • Both — you own an Indian company and a US company and run the whole chain. Best margin, but every cost and risk on this page is yours.

Best first move

Start on one side only. The gentlest way in is to be the US importer buying from an experienced Indian exporter (or the reverse) — let a seasoned partner carry the other half while you learn one side well.

Cost

$0 — it's a decision

Time

Think it through

Who does it

You

2

Register your company & get your licenses

In plain words

Before you can legally buy or move rice, you need a registered company and a set of licenses. Most are cheap and one-time. Skip one and your container can get stuck at a port. Here is every license — you only need the column for the side you chose in Step 1. The official “apply here” link for each one is in the Links section at the bottom.

INDIA Export side

To legally buy, process and export rice out of India

  • Company + PAN
    A registered business (proprietorship, LLP or Pvt Ltd) and its tax PAN. Everything else needs this first.
    ₹0–15,000 · 1–15 days
  • IEC — Import Export Code
    The master key. Nothing leaves India without it. One-time and lifetime (free yearly online confirmation keeps it active).
    DGFT · ₹500 · 1–3 days
  • GST number + LUT
    Tax registration to trade; a "LUT" letter lets you export without paying GST upfront.
    GST portal · free · ~7 days
  • FSSAI Central License
    Food-safety license, required for anyone handling or exporting food.
    FSSAI · ₹7,500/yr · 15–30 days
  • APEDA Registration (RCMC)
    Farm-export body membership. Mandatory to export rice.
    APEDA · ₹5,900 · 5-yr validity
  • AD Code (from your bank)
    Your bank's code, registered at the port online. Export paperwork can't be filed without it.
    Bank + port · small · 3–7 days
  • Export bank account
    A current account set up to receive foreign payments the legal way.
    Any bank · free · 2–7 days

USA Import side

To legally bring the rice in and clear it as food

  • US company + EIN
    An LLC to be the official importer and hold a bank account. EIN is the company's tax number.
    State + IRS · $50–500 · EIN free
  • FDA facility registration (your supplier does this)
    The Indian mill registers with the FDA and names a "US Agent." If they haven't, your shipment is refused. Get their number in writing.
    FDA · free · supplier's job
  • FSVP + DUNS number
    A file proving you checked your supplier meets US food-safety rules. DUNS is a free company ID used at the border.
    You keep it · free
  • FDA Prior Notice
    A heads-up to the FDA before every shipment arrives. Your broker files it.
    per shipment
  • USDA plant permit (PPQ 587)
    Permit to import a plant product. Apply about a month ahead.
    USDA-APHIS · low/no fee · ~30 days
  • Customs bond + Importer number
    Registers you with Customs and guarantees the duties. Buy per-shipment or a yearly bond.
    CBP · $100–700
  • A customs broker
    Not legally required, but you'll want one. They file everything and clear the goods for you.
    ~$150 per shipment

Don't miss this

The one first-timers forget: the FDA registration is on the Indian mill, not on you — but if your mill isn't FDA-registered with a valid US Agent, your rice is turned away at the US port. Confirm their FDA number before you pay any deposit.

Time

About 3–6 weeks

3

Find a buyer & agree the price

In plain words

Don't buy a container of rice and hope to sell it. Get the buyer first. Then agree the price — and pay attention to the Incoterm, the one short word that decides exactly who pays for what, and where one person's job ends and the other's begins.

The four price terms you'll hear

EXWEx-Works. Price is just the rice at the mill gate. You pay for everything after that. Cheapest sticker, most work.
FOBFree On Board. Seller delivers, clears export, and loads it onto the ship at an Indian port. You take over from there. The most common way rice is quoted.
CIFCost, Insurance & Freight. Seller pays the rice + shipping + insurance to the US port. You handle US customs onward.
DDPDelivered Duty Paid. Seller delivers to your US door, everything paid. Easiest for you, most expensive, and rare from Indian mills.

The golden rule

Get a signed order — ideally backed by a Letter of Credit (a bank payment guarantee) or a deposit — before you spend on rice. Then you're filling a real order, not gambling on inventory. It also tells you the exact grade, packing and price to buy.

Cost

Small (samples, outreach)

Time

Ongoing — start early

Who does it

You

4

Buy the rice

In plain words

Now buy the rice that matches your buyer's order. US buyers mostly want basmati (long, aromatic rice). Rice is sold by grade (the variety + how it's milled) and priced per metric ton (1 ton = 1,000 kg). These are the grades a US buyer will name:

Rough export prices, FOB an Indian port, USD per ton (2024–25 crop). Prices move with the harvest and the rupee — always get a live quote.
GradeTypePrice / ton
1121 BasmatiPremium, longest grain~$1,060
1718 BasmatiPremium~$955
1509 BasmatiMid, early crop~$840
SugandhaValue aromatic~$755
PR-11 / PR-14Non-basmati long grain~$560
  • Test the quality — moisture, broken-grain %, grain length, and (very important for the US) pesticide residue. Ask for a lab report.
  • Get a sample first — smell it, cook it, measure it before you commit to a container.
  • Choose packing — standard is 50 kg bags; or printed 1/5/10 kg retail bags if you're selling your own brand.
  • Pay a premium of about $60–120/ton for certified pesticide-residue-free rice — serious US buyers expect it.

Check the rules first

India controls rice exports for food security, and the rules change fast. Right now (late 2024 through 2026) basmati and white rice export freely — no minimum price, no export tax. But broken rice is still restricted, and any grade can change with one government notice. Confirm your grade's status on the DGFT website before you sign.

~$26,500 for one 20ft container of 1121

Time

Days once terms agreed

5

Pack it, treat it & clear it out of India

In plain words

The rice is bagged, loaded into a steel shipping container, treated against pests, inspected, and officially cleared by Indian customs to leave. A local shipping agent (called a CHA or freight forwarder) does most of the hands-on work — you supply documents and money.

  • Load & seal — the mill fills the container with bags; note the container and seal numbers.
  • Fumigation — a pest treatment; you get a fumigation certificate.
  • Phytosanitary certificate — a government plant inspection. For the US it must state the rice is "free of Khapra beetle" — without those exact words, the US can refuse it.
  • Shipping bill — the export declaration filed online; customs clears the rice to leave.
  • Bill of Lading — once the ship sails, the shipping line issues this. It's your proof of ownership of the cargo.

Cost

A few hundred $ in fees

Time

About 1–2 weeks

Who does it

Your CHA / forwarder + inspectors

6

Ship it across the ocean

In plain words

Your container goes on a ship from an Indian port to a US port. Because rice is heavy, one small 20ft container already holds about 25 tons — and that's the smart size to start with. A bigger 40ft box barely holds more rice but costs a lot more to ship.

ContainerRice it holds50 kg bagsWhy
20ft~25 tons500Fills up by weight — the practical choice for rice
40ft~27 tons540Only slightly more rice; extra space goes empty
  • Ocean freight: about $2,300–3,000 for a 20ft box, port to port (2026 rates on the main India–US routes).
  • Time at sea: roughly 30–35 days for a direct route.
  • Marine insurance (about 0.3–0.5% of the cargo value) is cheap and non-negotiable — a lost or wet container is a total loss otherwise.
  • Extra charges to expect: fuel surcharges, port handling both ends, and truck delivery at the US port (budget $600–1,000 US-side).

First-shipment tip

Ship one 20ft full container to start. It keeps your money at risk small, keeps the per-ton shipping cost sensible, and is enough to prove the whole chain works before you scale up.

Cost

~$2,500 freight + ~$120 insurance

Time

~30–35 days at sea

Who does it

Shipping line, via your forwarder

7

Clear US customs & pay duty + tariff

In plain words

When the ship arrives, three US agencies check your rice before it's allowed in. Your US customs broker handles all the paperwork. Then you pay a small fixed duty plus a bigger tariff — and that tariff is the number that changes most and matters most.

  • FDA (food safety) — wants your supplier's registration, the Prior Notice, and your FSVP file. Can inspect any food.
  • USDA (plants & pests) — wants the import permit and the "Khapra-beetle-free" phytosanitary certificate.
  • Customs / CBP (border & tax) — wants the entry form, your bond, the correct product code, and the money.

The product code for milled basmati is HTS 1006.30.90.59. The base duty is tiny — about 1.4¢ per kg (~$350 on a 25-ton load). The number that really moves your cost is the country tariff on India, added on top.

The number that changes — check it every single time

The US tariff on Indian goods has swung wildly: 0% before 2025 → 50% in Aug 2025 → ~18% under a Feb 2026 deal → reported ~10% by mid-2026, with rice left out of some of the relief. This one percentage can add hundreds of dollars per ton and kill a deal. Never price a shipment without confirming today's exact rate with your customs broker.

Cost

Duty + tariff, + ~$250 broker/bond, + ~$800 port & delivery

Time

A few days to clear

Who does it

Your US customs broker

8

Receive it & sell it

In plain words

Once cleared, a truck takes the container to your warehouse. Now you hand it to your buyer, or store, repack and sell it yourself. You get paid — and if you used a Letter of Credit, the bank releases the money once your documents check out.

  • Delivery — a short truck move from the port ("drayage") to your warehouse or your buyer's door.
  • Get paid — against the shipping documents (via the Letter of Credit) or on your agreed terms.
  • Then repeat — with a proven supplier, buyer and route, the second shipment is far easier than the first.

Trucking (part of Step 7 budget)

The honest test

Before spending on any license, get four real numbers: one FOB quote from an exporter, one freight quote, one tariff figure from a broker, and one wholesale price from a US buyer. If those leave you a margin you're happy with — then start. If they don't, you just saved yourself the whole setup cost.

Paperwork

Your forwarder and broker prepare most of these. Your job is to make sure each one exists and that they all match — a mismatch (say, the invoice says 500 bags but the bill of lading says 480) is a classic reason a container gets held.

Before you commit

The 5 things that sink first-timers

1. Tariff & policy surprises. US tariffs and India's export rules both change on short notice. A deal that works at 10% dies at 50%. Fix it: confirm today's US rate and India's current policy, in writing, before every contract.

2. Quality rejection at the port. The FDA can hold rice for pesticide residue or filth; USDA for pests. A refused container is nearly a total loss. Fix it: test before shipping, use a clean supplier, and get a third-party inspection before the container is sealed.

3. Getting cheated on payment. New suppliers and buyers are both unproven. Fix it: use a Letter of Credit or a deposit, verify the other side (see the vetting checklist), and start small.

4. Port delays. Slow clearance racks up expensive storage fees ("demurrage"). Fix it: a good broker, complete documents filed early, and a few days' buffer in your budget.

5. Running out of money. People budget the rice price and forget freight, tariff, fees, and the weeks your cash is tied up in transit. Fix it: use the full cost model above, add 15–20% buffer, and make sure you can float one whole container plus the ~6–8 weeks before you're paid.
